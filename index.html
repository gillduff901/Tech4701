Terms of Use
Terms of my use.

You have agreed to a few hundred Terms of Use in your life. You read none of them. Each one was written by someone else, about a tool, to protect the people who made the tool.

A Philosophy of Use is the reverse. You write it. It's about you. It protects what you care about. And unlike the fine print, it only counts if you could actually break it.

This course has asked you to think two ways at once: rhetorically (what human situation does this output have to enter?) and computationally (what system behavior does my prompt shape?). A Philosophy of Use is where those two habits turn around and look at you. The situation is your career. The system is you.

Five clauses, five short games, about fifteen minutes. Each clause ends with one sentence in your own words and a wax seal. At the end, a machine assembles your sentences into a first draft and tells you what it thinks of you. Its reading will be crude. That's the point: your Philosophy of Use is what you write in the gap between the machine's reading and what you actually mean.

There is no score. There is a draft. Nothing you type leaves this page.

Clause I · Orient

The Baggage Claim

What values and intellectual commitments do I bring to AI?

Nobody arrives at a tool empty-handed. You bring values you learned long before you typed a prompt: from your family, your field, the teacher who marked you down, the job where you got it wrong. The belt is running. Claim three. Click a bag to claim it; click again to put it back.

    The Orient clause is not a list of virtues. Anyone can claim craft and speed and rigor. Your philosophy is the ranking: which one you drop when the clock is on you, and whether you're willing to say so in writing.

    Be specific to your field. "I'll fact-check everything" is not a sentence anyone has ever lived by.
    The Discern clause is not a promise to verify everything; you won't. It's a map of the edge of your own competence, and a rule for what you do when an output lands past that edge. The feeling of rightness is identical on both sides of the line. Only the checking differs.

    Clause III · Relate

    The Group Chat

    How should AI affect my relationships and collaborations?

    AI doesn't enter your life alone. It enters your group project, your team, your inbox. Here's a chat with a teammate. Reply as the person you are on a Tuesday night, not the one you'd like to be.

    Taylor · project partner

    Say it to their face

    Pick one of the three replies you sent. Would you say it out loud, in person, to someone whose opinion of you matters?

    A rule, not a vibe. Someone should be able to catch you breaking it.
    The Relate clause is about other people's work and other people's trust. It has to answer three questions your teammates will otherwise answer for you: who gets told that AI was used, who gets asked before their work is touched, and whose name goes on the result.

    Clause IV · Value

    The Delegation Dial

    What is the significance of expertise, experience, and human judgment?

    You audited autonomy for an agent. Now audit it for yourself. Seven tasks. Set each dial from I do it to AI does it and I don't look. Be honest about an ordinary week, not your best day.

    I do itAI suggests, I writeAI drafts, I editAI does, I checkAI does, I don't look
    0 of 7 set
    "Humans are still important" is a platitude. Say what your judgment is for, in your work.
    The Value clause says what human judgment is for in your work, not in general. The strongest version names a task, names the experience that qualifies you, and names what the tool can't see from inside the prompt. Expertise is the thing you're supposed to be accumulating; the clause says how you'll keep accumulating it with a very fast draft always on offer.

    Clause V · Care

    Who Else Is in the Room

    What responsibilities and obligations constrain my choices?

    You pasted something into a chatbot. It was a normal Tuesday. The chat window looked private: you, the cursor, the box. Let's see who was actually in the room. For each one, decide: does this person bind you, or not?

    A constraint that costs you nothing isn't a constraint.
    The Care clause is the only one with teeth. It names what you owe and to whom, and it's only convincing if keeping it sometimes costs you something: time, a grade, a shortcut, a client who wanted magic. The test is the empty room. What do you still do there?

    The breakability test

    A philosophy is only a philosophy if you could violate it. For each clause: can you picture an ordinary day on which you'd break it?

    You now have five sentences, three crude readings, and a list of what the machine flagged. None of it is your Philosophy of Use. The document you'll write is longer, in your voice, and answers the five questions in an order that makes sense for your field. Use this draft the way the course has taught you to use any output: keep what survives your reading, repair what doesn't, and be able to say which is which.
